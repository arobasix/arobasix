Road to Geneva — risk analysis

Hollerith Technology, IBM Dehomag and the Nazi State

A historical case study on data, administration and political power.

During the last century, Hollerith technology, through IBM and its German subsidiary Dehomag, provided census and administrative tools used by the Nazi state. It contributed to the administrative efficiency of persecution, but it was neither the origin of Nazi ideology nor, by itself, the technical cause of the Holocaust.

Today, the equivalent would be interconnected databases covering civil records, tax and medical files, telecommunications, social networks, video surveillance, biometrics and geolocation.

In a totalitarian regime, such systems could be used to:

  • identify an ethnic, religious or political minority;
  • locate opponents, journalists and activists;
  • cross-reference addresses, movements, employment and communications;
  • block access to work, healthcare, banking or transport;
  • organise arrests, deportations or forced labour;
  • generate lists of “suspects” through automated profiling.

Essential democratic protections

  • collect only necessary data;
  • prohibit reuse for unrelated purposes;
  • separate statistical and named records;
  • limit ethnic, religious and political data;
  • require encryption, access logs, limited retention and correction rights;
  • place police and intelligence services under independent oversight.

Technology is not the only safeguard. The real protection lies in plural institutions and a genuine ability to resist state power.
